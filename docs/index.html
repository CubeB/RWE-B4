Orders and unit behaviour

  • Buildings stand in the water rather than on it. A building whose yardmap has any land cell in it is levelled onto the lowest terrain corner under those cells; one whose yardmap is all water — every shipyard, tidal generator, sonar station, torpedo launcher and floating radar — stands at seaLevel − waterline. The placement box now asks the same function the building will, because in the original the two are the same number rather than merely the same rule
  • A wreck on the sea bed blocks a hovercraft, and that is faithful rather than a bug — a play-test expected otherwise and the binary disagrees. The map square holds a feature type index, never an instance, so a wreck's own height is unreachable from any collision test even in principle; all five readers of the blocking bit reject the square before a single movement-class or unit field is read, with no exemption for canhover. What makes it visible is the data: every hovercraft leaves a blocking=1 corpse, where a naval corpse is a flat blocking=0 plate, so a hover battle obstructs the sea bed and a naval one does not
  • Submarines no longer appear at the shipyard's build‑piece height and drop on their first step: a mobile ground or sea unit is settled onto the terrain as it is created
  • A pad is taken from the moment someone sets out for it, not from the moment they arrive, and stays taken until that aircraft has been repaired and has left — so a squadron coming home off one raid spreads over the pads it has instead of piling onto one. Switching a pad off turns away new arrivals but does not recall the aircraft already coming: the query walks only switched-on pads, while VTOL_Landing's re-test on the way in asks whether the pad is free (“Landing aborted: no pads available”) and never asks about the switch. It needs no new saved state — the claim is the landing order itself
  • Air repair pads work in both directions. A damaged aircraft on patrol, guard or an attack mission breaks off for a pad and resumes the order underneath when it is mended; the pad mends whatever is parked on it as the work it does when idle. An idle damaged aircraft still lands where it stands — VTOL_Standby carries no health test, so only a plane with something to do goes home
  • Abandoned nanoframes rot. One energy‑point of the frame's own build cost per tick, which is why a Solar melts in twenty‑five seconds and a half‑built fusion plant takes twenty minutes. The construction display runs backwards on its own, because the same value drives it. At zero the frame removes itself with no wreck and no death script — and a unit killed while still under construction leaves nothing to reclaim either
  • Thunders and Shadows bomb what their patrol route takes them past. A deliberate divergence: they ship StandingFireOrder=0 where the Phoenix and Hurricane ship 2, and the patrol engage check is Fire At Will exactly, so a Hold Fire bomber is now seeded to Fire At Will and takes the path the Phoenix already took
  • Reclaim features and units, with progress shared between builders and resources credited as work is done
  • Repair, patrol (engages en route, single click patrols between two points) and capture orders, each with panel button and cursor
  • Self‑destruct countdown (Ctrl‑D, 5 s, cancellable) using the unit's SelfDestructAs weapon
  • Transports: load and unload orders with TA's own cursors and panel buttons; carried units ride the transport's QueryTransport piece and are set down on clear ground
  • Bombers fly through the target on their run, release when the timing is right, and turn back after a short run‑out or at the map edge instead of flying off the map
  • Bombers stop missing. A run used to take its heading from whatever velocity the aircraft happened to have when it committed, then steer at a point beyond the target along that line; since committing tolerates twenty degrees of error, the aim point sat up to ninety units to one side — wider than the bombsight’s across-track gate. Every pass after the first crossed the target 78–130 units wide and never opened the sight. The original recomputes the line through the target every tick, so it cannot drift; with the real ARMTHUND.FBI numbers this went from three bombs dropped in two minutes to forty-two, and from two hits to twenty-eight or more, from every bearing
  • Gunships work a standoff ring instead of flying bomber runs. HoverAttack=1 is set on exactly two units in the whole game — the Brawler and the Rapier — and the flag had never been read, so a Brawler spiralled in to half a unit of its target. The original gives them their own mission: close to weapon range, then hold a ring of two thirds of that range (246 units for a Brawler, 300 for a Rapier) and step exactly 45° around it, alternating side on every arrival, so it settles into a two-point shuttle rather than an orbit
  • …and it holds its nose on the target while it does so, sliding sideways between stations. Both gunship weapons say turret=0, so the gun is bolted to the hull and only bears within about a third of a right angle of straight ahead; pointing the nose along the flight path instead left it crossing the target at better than sixty degrees off, dealing 480 damage over a stretch where it should deal 2600. Facing the target is both what the swing looks like from the ground and the only way the gun can bear
  • …and it engages of its own accord. The ring was only ever reached from an explicit attack order or from a patrol engaging en route, so a gunship left to fire at will shot from wherever it stood — and since an aircraft with no orders goes looking for somewhere to land, it broke off mid-fight and set down. A free aircraft that can see something in weapon range now gets an attack order, which is what the original’s idle handler does: it searches at sight range and turns what it finds into a mission
  • A gun cannot shoot at a submerged submarine, and a torpedo cannot shoot at a tank. Which of the two rules applies turns on the weapon’s waterweapon flag, and “out of the water” is measured to the top of the model rather than to its origin — which is what makes a surfaced submarine shootable by everything and a submerged one shootable only by torpedoes. A hovercraft rides with half its model proud of the surface, and that is what puts it out of a torpedo’s reach
  • The search that breaks a unit off its patrol, and the one an idle aircraft uses to pick a fight, now run the same eligibility test as everything else. They ran none at all, so an idle gunship carrying an anti-air weapon would break off and chase a tank
  • Order buttons are offered only where the unit’s own FBI says they apply — all fifteen of them, across the whole selection rather than only when one unit is picked. A mixed selection offers a button if any unit supports it, which is what the original does. Four of the flags behind them are read here for the first time, and one of them, canreclamate, gates reclaim and repair in the simulation as well: it turns out the parser mirrors the bit and the repair check reads the copy, so a factory or a carrier can no longer reclaim and repair merely because it has a WorkerTime
  • Ships and shipyards can be placed and built in water (a missing MaxWaterDepth now means any depth); torpedoes only hit units in the water and run under the surface
  • Aircraft move off and steer while climbing out, and bombers drop a stick of three per pass
  • An attack run holds speed and banks at the aircraft's own turn rate, so the turn back for another pass is an arc of radius speed / turnRate rather than a stop and a pivot; the run-out is sized from that radius, and a run is only committed to when the aircraft is close and lined up — one caught inside its own turn circle extends away first instead of orbiting and missing
  • Aircraft set down on dry land only, and an order given while one is landing aborts the descent and sends it back up
  • A building settles inside the arc its own FBI names rather than a flat ten degrees either way. The spread is per-unit and covers a factor of thirty-two — a vehicle plant barely moves at 1024, a light laser tower swings the full 32768 — and the shipyards and aircraft plants name none at all, which is what keeps their roll-off square. The factory exemption we had was therefore backwards: the data already held the yards square while the kbot labs and vehicle plants were being straightened in error. The two Fortification Walls ask for zero, and the original’s random number generator returns without advancing for anything under two, which is why a wall line comes out dead straight
  • Construction aircraft have to be flying to use the fabricator, and work the original's pattern: their goal sits one BuildDistance from the job and steps a seventh of a turn round it every five seconds — seven stations, nose held on the job — so the movement is a dash, a wait, another dash. They lathe throughout
  • Aircraft bank because they are accelerating sideways, not because an animation says so: tan(roll) = BankScale × lateral acceleration / gravity through a one-pole lag, straight out of the original, with no rate limit or maximum angle
  • Aircraft fly the original's arrival profile rather than bang–bang throttle: drag, then steer for sqrt(2 × Acceleration × distance) toward the target, moving at most one tick's acceleration, with the range floored at eight units so the last few are a straight run-down. The law it replaces was full throttle or full brakes with nothing in between, and the two modes chattered against each other whenever an aircraft sat near its destination — which is what made construction aircraft shake on station, since bank is computed from change in velocity. Bank sign changes over a work orbit fell from 71 to 17
  • Aircraft thrusters emit TA's SFXTYPE_VTOL exhaust. Disassembling TotalA.exe settled what it actually draws: the flamestream sequence from FX.GAF, one frame per tick, drifting a sixth of the thruster vector each tick for six ticks — so each puff swells from a two-pixel speck into a ragged yellow flame as it falls, and the plume is a graded line with the largest furthest from the nozzle. Two things were in the way: the SFX type was an unhandled TODO, and the script starts its flame loop from MoveRate1, which the engine never called
  • A builder that raised its arm and then did nothing was losing its build stance to a stow that finished after the redeploy; starting a job now waits for the stow, and moving from one job to the next keeps the arm out
  • Aircraft count as arrived within a few ticks of travel rather than 8 units — they cannot stop dead, and the tighter figure left orders that never finished and new aircraft hovering over the pad. Landing keeps the tight figure, refuses water, and retries elsewhere if the spot is taken
  • Units report arrival at the end of the journey, not at every shift-queued waypoint
  • Sea transports load through their own scripts — doors open, the crane swings out and hooks the unit, stows it in the hold — and the Atlas hovers down to lift; LOAD/BLAST/CLOAK buttons appear only where they apply
  • The crane takes one unit at a time and is not free until it has stowed it; one unload order sets down one unit; cargo has no hitbox, stays off the minimap, forgets its old orders and dies with the transport — credited, now, to whoever killed the transport
  • Transports run the original's rules in full. The hover transports (Bear, Turtle) work — they are crane transports like the Hulk, and RWE had routed them down the aircraft path — capacities come from the key the 3.1 exe actually reads (Bear/Turtle/Envoy 5, Hulk 20; the wiki's 6/6/20 are a dead 1.0-era key's values), air transports carry exactly one whatever their FBI claims, a landed aircraft may ride, nothing submerged may be lifted, sea and hover transports refuse ships and subs, and an air transport can set anything down on water that can live there
  • Fighters prefer the air. The decision to break off and fight runs the same chooser as the gun itself — the original has no second search — so a Freedom Fighter with NOTAIR as its bad-target set takes the aircraft while there is one and only strafes tanks under an empty sky
  • …and when it does strafe, it flies the original’s pass rather than the bomber’s: take the target, overshoot to three weapon ranges, break ninety degrees left or right at random for one weapon range, come round again, holding the aim throughout. It is gated to ground targets, because a dogfight is a different mission in the original and the overshoot would be worse than the generic pattern for one
  • Wreckage and scenery have hit points from the feature's damage: blasts break them apart into their featuredead form, and reclaim time follows what is left of them
  • The TDF reader takes the files the original actually ships. Running it over all 800 .tdf/.fbi/.ota/.gui files in the game data found three it refused; one was ours, a top-level block closed with };, which UNITVIEW.TDF and Translate.tdf both do. Parse failures also say where they happened now: the line and column were recorded and then kept private with no way to read them, and the wanted character was printed as a number, so a broken mod file reported “Expected 91” and nothing else
  • COB VM fixes: script call arguments were passed in reverse, packed positions unpacked unsigned, attach-unit/drop-unit implemented
  • Units only auto‑target what their owner can see or has on radar; a new order interrupts building cleanly; shift‑click removes a planned building
  • Units choose what to shoot at the way the original does. RWE took the first enemy by unit id in range, so a dozen units all volleyed at the same unfortunate Peewee. The original scores every candidate rand(dx² + dz²) and takes the lowest — the nearest usually wins but not always, and that is the whole of TA’s spread of fire. A weapon’s bad target category does not veto a candidate, it only holds it back as a fallback, so a Samson with no aircraft about still shoots tanks. ShootMe, absent on exactly the fifty-four passive buildings, is why nothing wanders over to an enemy metal extractor and opens up on it — computer players are exempt, so the AI still does. Categories turned out not to be ids at all: each category name owns a 512-bit set of unit type indices, which is also why the original cannot carry more than 512 unit types
  • A gun bolted to the hull waits until the hull is pointing the right way. turret=0 — 38 of the 170 shipped weapons, and not the tank guns anyone would guess: aircraft weapons, torpedoes, vertical-launch missiles and bombs — makes the original refuse the shot rather than turn the unit, measuring the bearing against the unit’s own heading. Vertical launches and bombs are exempt, having handlers that never ask which way the unit faces
  • Units are built on the standing orders their own FBI names instead of everything being Fire At Will and Maneuver. The move order barely matters — 117 of the units that name one say Maneuver, which is what was hard-coded — but thirteen say hold fire, and those are the artillery, the silos, the mines and the bombers that had been going hunting
  • Damaged units smoke. It turned out RWE already had the effect and never reached it: the trigger is not in the engine at all but in each unit’s own COB script, a SmokeUnit thread that is word-for-word identical in 186 of the 200 shipped scripts. It starts below 66% health and puffs every max(health%×50, 200) milliseconds, so a nearly dead unit smokes every sixth tick, and each puff rolls its colour against the same health figure rather than switching from light to heavy at a threshold
  • Return Fire does something. It was a second name for hold fire — the enum, the button and the protocol message all existed but acquisition was gated on fire-at-will alone. In the original it lives in the damage path, and the only difference from fire at will is that the retaliation test accepts firing mode 1 or 2 where the hunting scan demands 2
  • Missiles have a motor. Every projectile flew at a constant weaponVelocity while weaponacceleration, startvelocity, selfprop, twophase, vlaunch and flighttime sat unread. The original launches at startvelocity, adds weaponacceleration every tick and treats weaponvelocity as a cap; the burn lasts range / weaponvelocity ticks and running out is not death — without burnblow the missile keeps its speed and coasts under gravity, which is why one fired at the far edge of its range arrives unguided. Steering is gated on guidance, not tracks, and the turn rate in the TDF is per second: we were applying it raw, thirty times too fast, which snapped every tracking missile straight onto its target. A vertical launch leaves the tube pointing up with no velocity at all, so the whole climb comes out of the motor
  • Damage is computed the way the original computes it. Blast falloff is quadratic, not linear — (1 − d/r)² × (1 − E) + E — so half way out a blast pays a quarter where we were paying a half, and edgeeffectiveness turns out to be the multiplier at the rim, which is how a Flak gun keeps nine tenths of its bite right out to the edge. Anything with an areaofeffect of sixteen or less skips the curve entirely for full damage, which covers forty-three of the hundred and thirty-six shipped weapons including the twenty-eight machine guns and light lasers at eight. damagemodifier is armour, applied while a unit’s script says it is armoured and ignored outright by any hit of 30000 or more
  • TA has hidden veterancy, and it works both ways: a unit deals 6% more damage per tier and takes 4% less, five kills to a tier and five tiers at most. Only the defender’s half was known; implementing that alone would have made every veteran strictly weaker. The COB GET VETERAN_LEVEL query returns nothing at all in the original, so the tier we report is the engine’s own rather than the scheme invented here earlier
  • Units rock with recoil when they fire, and now away from the shot rather than into it. The shot direction is turned into the unit’s own frame before it reaches the RockUnit script, and a half turn was being added on top of that — a correction that belongs to XZAtan’s own return value, not to script arguments generally. Every one of the six geometry checks in the new test comes out exactly inverted on the old code
  • …and only the seventeen units whose scripts define RockUnit rock at all, by a flat 800 rather than an angle scaled from the weapon’s damage. The engine asks every unit and the script is the whole filter, so a Peewee no longer rocks. Bombs never rock either
  • Pathing: slope‑aware speed and routing, no corner squeezing between obstacles, unreachable destinations end at the closest reachable point
  • Builders no longer get stuck on unreachable build sites; nanoframes killed while unattended die cleanly

Interface, media and the front end

  • Holding SHIFT over a cloaked unit draws a white ring at its minimum cloaking radius — the v3.1 patch feature, projected onto the ground rather than facing the camera
  • Save and load. The whole simulation round-trips through a save file — units with their COB virtual machines mid-thought, projectiles mid-flight, spreading fires, the economy, the random number generator — proven by the hash harness: save, load into a fresh sim, identical hash, then a hundred further ticks of both with identical hashes after every one. The F2 menu's Save Game and Load Game use the original's own panels with a working name box, Load Game sits on the single-player menu too, and saves are .rwesave files in the local data directory. One documented divergence: AI players re-plan after a load
  • The movies play. The GOG release ships them disguised as Data/*.ZRB — ordinary Smacker files inside. RWE grew its own SMK2 decoder, validated byte-for-byte against FFmpeg over all five films, and the INTRO button now shows the real intro, scaled to the window, any key to skip
  • Music, the original's way. The soundtrack (the tagged OST, in music/) plays situationally as the exe dictates: peace tracks by default, war drums when a scoring ring over the last thirty seconds of combat crosses the decoded thresholds — and only past thirty owned units — standing down after a quiet minute with a 1.2 s fade and four seconds of hush. The menu is silent bar the [BGM] ambience, as the original; Victory/Defeat track types exist in the binary and are triggered by nothing, so RWE invents no trigger
  • The in-game menu. Tab or F2 opens the original's GAME OPTIONS panel over the left sidebar, pausing the game; Options inside it opens the in-game options composite (PREFS + the RT pages with their commongui backgrounds); Exit leads to Exit to Menu / Exit Game
  • Options that work, front end and in game: sound and music volume (live), music on/off, unit shadows, screen scroll speed (25–200%), window mode (bordered / borderless / fullscreen) on the VISUALS page, game speed on the slider in game, a sound test — all persisted to rwe.cfg with OK/Cancel/Undo/Restore semantics; gadgets with nothing behind them are greyed, not faked
  • The speech console. Self-destruct counts down "Commander: five" in the top-left in COMIX.FNT — the words, the %s: %s format and the spoken count5–count0 recordings are all the original's — cancel prints "Self destruct terminated", cloaking prints "Cloaked"/"Visible", and a defeated player gets "Side forces have been obliterated" in their own colour
  • The exact interface colours, recovered from the binary: the "unwritable" colour table turned out to be written from a different base — a runtime nearest-match of GUIPAL.PAL into the screen palette. Cursor box bright green/red, a selected owner's queued box bright green over dark cyan (dark line inside), other builders' blue over navy, minimap jammer rings orange-red
  • Minimap detection rings (radar, sonar, jammers — sight deliberately excluded, as the exe does) and the anti-missile coverage ring (radius coverage − 512, white, dashed and chasing while the launcher has a round in the magazine and solid while it is empty), both clipped to the minimap as the exe clips them; the marching waypoint trail (the pathicon star every 48 units, sliding one spacing per second); the placement sweep (ten ticks, four lines crossing to the centre and back)
  • The unit info panel, in full. The footer describes the unit under the cursor — logo, name, health, metal at one decimal place and energy at none (the exe's own format strings), all four rates clamped at zero, the kills line under the damage bar with - Veteran from the fifth kill, the mission line taken from the original's own mission tables (Standby, Nanolathing, Under construction, Annihilating…), and a second name-and-bar slot showing whatever the current order is pointed at: the nanoframe a builder is raising and how far along it is, or Weapon and a percentage for a silo
  • The focused control and the selected row. A text box draws the exe's blue caret only while it holds the focus — the only focus indicator in the whole gadget renderer — and a list box brightens its selected row after drawing the text, matching the measured 1.84× of the palette table the original runs the row through
  • Window modes and resize: bordered (draggable, resizable), borderless and fullscreen; the UI keeps its size and the world grows; every scene follows a resize including the movie player
  • "Bot bot boom boom v1.0" under the main-menu title — B4 for short, being four B’s — and the version tag to match

Visibility

  • Radar no longer out‑reaches its own ring. The rule has two halves and only one was implemented: the altitude bonus is the detector's own height, and an outer cap of max(RadarDistance, SonarDistance) is applied before it. No shipped unit has more sonar than radar while having any radar, so on real data radar reaches exactly RadarDistance — which is what the minimap draws. A Peeper had been detecting at about 1060 against a 700 ring
  • A unit off the edge of the map still sees the map. Aircraft went dark the moment they crossed the edge on an attack run, because both reveal routines discarded the whole stamp when the centre cell was off the grid — a test that was RWE's own invention. UpdateUnitLOS (0x4827B0) has no bounds check on the unit's position anywhere in it; sight is a stamp, and one falling partly outside its destination is clipped, not dropped. The subtle half was that deleting the guard alone would not have worked: the ray walk breaks on leaving the grid, which is right for a centre inside the map but leaves a unit outside it blind, since every one of its rays starts outside and walks inward
  • Line of sight, fog of war and radar in the deterministic sim; unseen enemies hidden, radar contacts as blips, unexplored features hidden
  • Sight is blocked by higher ground, so hills cast shadows
  • Fog of war rebuilt from the original: the grid is indexed in TA's projected space, sight walks the authored ray fan from los.tdf with a running-horizon shadow cast and an 8-cell cap, the height grid holds a reveal/occlude pair, and the ragged edge is TA's own fog.gaf artwork picked by marching squares rather than procedural noise. Radar has no grid at all — it is a range query that finds units and reveals no ground
  • Fog drawn the way TA draws it: remembered ground in grey, unknown ground black, and the boundary its own hand-drawn fog.gaf frames, with the variant fixed in world space so the edge does not shimmer when the map scrolls
  • Fog meets the map border flush. Cells off the edge used to read as clear, so the ragged frame there had no neighbouring tile to cover it and a strip of map showed through along the top and left — the two sides the camera can actually reach
  • Minimap only reveals what the player can see or detect
  • Radar contacts belong on the minimap and nowhere else. RWE drew a coloured dot and a white box outline in the 3D world for every contact, which is an invention: the original’s world render is gated on a predicate that never reads the radar bits, so a radar-only contact is not drawn there and cannot be clicked. The minimap is the only reader, and it draws a contact exactly as it draws a unit you can see
  • A cloaked unit looks cloaked. The original composites the whole unit into a private bitmap and then picks its blitter on the cloak flag, the cloaked one running every pixel through a 64K “ALPHA TABLE” that averages the unit with whatever is behind it — an even half blend, not a stipple and not a palette ramp on the model. Its own side sees that ghost; an enemy sees nothing at all, and there is no partial reveal up close, since the proximity rule drops the cloak itself rather than thinning it. RWE blends with alpha and a depth prepass instead of the lookup table, which is a different mechanism for the same look
  • …and an enemy’s cloaked unit is no longer drawn at all. It was still rendered, shadowed, clickable and able to be nanolathed while the simulation was already refusing to target it
  • Nukes and anti-nukes can be built and fired. The magazine, the cost ramp and firing from stock all worked, but nothing could queue a round: the button is not on the orders panel at all, it is a gadget on the launcher’s own build page — ARMSILO1.GUI flags it in a shipped comment — and RWE only ever read GUI pages for builders, while all six launchers declare Builder=0. An anti-nuke now intercepts: it is gated on coverage, a square half-extent tested against the incoming missile’s aim point rather than its position, and the kill is that an interceptor’s blast detonates every projectile inside its own area of effect
  • A damaged aircraft flies to a repair pad rather than setting down where it stands. A pad counts only while it is switched on, the search is flat distance with height ignored, and where two qualify the winner is drawn at random — including the original’s habit of not advancing its generator at all when there is only one candidate. And the pad now mends it: one with no orders of its own nanolathes the nearest landed, damaged aircraft of its owner inside its build reach
  • Jammers, stealth and cloaking. A jammer clears the radar-contact bit for everything inside its radius — it hides units rather than making false ones — and it jams allies but not its owner; a stealth unit is never a contact at all; and cloak is a per-second energy drain, five times as much moving, that fails all-or-nothing rather than part-paying. An enemy inside MinCloakDistance starts a three-second fuse rather than refusing the cloak outright
  • Allies share their eyes. The lobby has had a team column all along and dropped it on the way into the game. A unit now reveals line of sight and contributes its radar and sonar to every player on its own team, so an ally's scouts light your map and an ally's dishes fill your radar picture — which is what an alliance is for. Jammers are left alone deliberately: the original jams everyone but the jammer's own owner, allies included, and that was already decoded and matched
  • The skirmish options reach the simulation. All five buttons cycled and only Difficulty ever did anything. Mapping: Mapped starts every player's grid explored but unlit, so the map draws from memory in grey while a unit standing on it stays hidden. Line of Sight: Circular stamps a flat disc instead of walking the ray tables, and Permanent promotes the explored grid into the visible grid — that single promotion is the whole option, because the fog renderer, unit visibility, detection and the AI's threat map all read the visible grid, so Permanent + Mapped falls out as "everything visible and coloured" with no special case. Radar is untouched in all three modes, deliberately: it never was elevation-aware. The help line under the setup box is TA's own text, all nine strings sitting in one run in the binary and listed for translation in Translate.tdf
  • …and the other two. Start Location: Random shuffles the players across the map's own StartPos entries by Fisher-Yates on the simulation's generator, so every peer deals the same hand and no coordinate is invented; Fixed draws nothing at all. Commander death, continuing, eliminates a player who has no living unit and lost one this tick — the second half is not padding, since the pass runs before the dead are deleted, and without it a player not yet given a unit is wiped on the first tick

Effects

  • The D‑gun leaves a trail. Not a beam — noexplode means the round detonates without being consumed, so it keeps its velocity and blasts again on the next cell: up to thirty‑six full‑strength blasts strung along its whole 240‑unit flight, which is why it is good against a clump. It also always flies the full range regardless of where the target was. Its reload now carries the health and veterancy scaling, and the round no longer lives 120 ticks instead of 36 because weapontimer was consulted before the range
  • Construction display taken from the exe: the original keeps a height per pixel and remaps the model against a moving threshold, so a bare line sweeps down twice, a solid silhouette grows up from the base, the texture follows it, and one last line sweeps back down over the finished model. Both greens are triangle waves over palette entries 160–175 at different rates, offset per unit — and the wireframe is the second of them, not the green → white → black cycle guessed at before. Shadow kept but cut out under the frame
  • Nano spray to match: four world units a tick, each particle stepping a place along palette entries 161–167 every tick and wrapping, so the stream shimmers along its length instead of each particle being dyed one colour for life; it fans across the middle three elevenths of the target. Reclaiming a feature runs two emitters, which is why a reclaim stream reads twice as thick
  • Burning features: fire‑starting weapons ignite trees and foliage, fires spread and leave burnt remains, using each feature's own burn sequence
  • Weapons do not damage wreckage or scenery; features keep hit points only to set how long they take to reclaim
  • Building deaths run the COB Killed script: pieces fly off with smoke and fire trails and impact explosions
  • Reclaimed features play their golden dissolve sequence
  • The solar collector's creased panels were triangulation, not bad UVs: the original scan-converts whole quads, so skewed textured quads now tessellate as bilinear patches. Selection plates are no longer drawn as model geometry, and textured non-quads are dropped as the original drops them
  • The original does light its models after all, and the earlier finding here — no normals, no sun, texels at 1.0 — was reading the SHADING=off rasterizer. 0x458744 picks between two complete chains on that one bit and the option defaults on. The shaded chain computes a level per vertex, (int)(5.0 × dot(n, sun)) & 0x1F, and Gouraud-interpolates the integer row across the face; the sun vector is deliberately not a unit vector, which is what sets the ramp at thirteen rows rather than thirty-two. RWE had computed it per pixel from a flat face normal, which cannot ramp at all
  • …and the & 0x1F wrap is kept, because it is where the original's contrast comes from. In TA the solar collector's right panel is not darker than the left, it is solid black beside a washed-out one — row 1 against row 28. A centred, monotone ramp gave 1.94× and 0.68×: two panels that read the same, which is exactly what the play-test complained of
  • …and normals are averaged per polygon, not per triangle. A textured quad is a 4×4 bilinear patch here and colour polygons are fanned, so averaging over the engine's own triangles weights a polygon by how many pieces it was cut into and double-counts the corners a fan diagonal touches — enough to push one corner of that panel from row 0 to row −1, which the mask turns into row 31: the brightest row in the table on the darkest face of the model. They are built in meshFrom3do now, where the polygons are still whole, and agree with the byte-verified reference to the row
  • …and a row is not worth what the table's generator says. PALETTE.SHD stores palette indices from a nearest-neighbour search, so its bright half runs out of palette to move into and saturates: row 16 lands at 1.07 and row 31 at 1.55, where the linear reading claims 1.10 and 2.13. RWE was over-brightening every lit face while wrapped faces sat near black — measured against a screenshot of the original, 39.8% of a solar collector's pixels below luminance 8 against the original's 25.1%. A two-segment fit to the measurements halves the contrast between the lit and shadowed panels, from about 11:1 to 5.4:1
  • …and the row is read from the table now, not from a curve. PALETTE.SHD stores palette indices from a nearest‑neighbour search, so a row does not scale a colour — it snaps the scaled colour back onto the 256 entries that exist. At row 31 the real table leaves eight of those entries exactly where they were and puts fifty‑eight of them on white, where the fitted curve claimed a uniform 1.807× for every one of them. Each texel's palette index now rides through the texture atlas in a second single‑channel copy, and the fragment reads SHD[row × 256 + texel] straight out of a 256×32 image of the table — the original's inner loop entire: one shift, one add, one byte load, no second mask and no blend between rows. Two things were measured before any of it was written: a C++ transcription of the generator at 0x4BADF0 reproduces the shipped table byte for byte, so a data set missing the file gets a real table rather than a curve; and the cheap version of the change — a per‑texel ratio applied to the mipmapped colour, which would have kept the atlas's filtering for free — loses up to 119/255 across 38 palette entries, because a channel that is zero before the row cannot be scaled into one that is not
  • A unit's shadow is its own silhouette, not a projection. The developer who wrote TA's graphics engine described unit shadows as the unit's cached bitmap "rendered it offset from the unit", and buildings as the only things he "decided to go ahead and properly project". The binary agrees — there are two passes, and the one a unit takes copies the drawable header and then rep movs its pixels with no transform of any kind, where the building path sizes a rectangle and rep stos fills it with a single byte. RWE had the building treatment on everything, so a tank's shadow stretched the way a factory's does. It has both now: a mobile unit's shadow is one displacement of its own outline, which carries the silhouette across unchanged because the world projection is orthographic. The original's second shadow bit came too, as the vehicle-shadows key — turn it off and the tanks lose their shadows while the factories keep theirs
  • The shading strengths were never turned up. Units were being shaded at a quarter strength and buildings at two fifths, left over from an early softening, while three comments claimed both defaulted to full and that "what ships is what the original draws". All eight defaults are 100 now — without which three quarters of the exact table above would not have been visible on a unit
  • Wreckage sinks. The corpse spawns at the dying unit's exact height on land and at sea alike — no waterline and no clamp — and what the water branch adds is a velocity: a fixed 0.175 units a tick downward, spent by a per-tick sweep, when the ground beneath is at or below sea level. Deep water on the shipped naval maps is 55 to 85 units, so an ocean wreck sinks for ten to sixteen seconds and one in the shallows is gone in under half a second. The clearest evidence the rule is deliberate is its exemption: a unit whose FBI says IsFeature is skipped, and the two that matter are the floating dragon's teeth

Skirmish AI

  • Scouting: a Peeper or Fink from the air plant flies three‑leg routes to the ground seen least recently, keeping clear of known guns; a scout vehicle from the vehicle plant and, until they exist, a raider standing in; hurt scouts head home
  • Transports: the base floods the heightmap to learn what its kbots can walk to; when there is metal across the water an Atlas or Valkyrie is built and ferries a constructor over to run an outpost, and in the attack phase it carries the army to a landing near an enemy it cannot walk to
  • Perception through fog of war, threat map, expansion build order (solars, extractors on real patches, lab, radar, towers, metal makers), factories, constructors that assist
  • Single‑army rally, defend and attack cycle; Easy / Standard / Hard / Brutal profiles via --ai-difficulty
  • Debug panel and 30‑second status lines in rwe.log; verified live on Coast To Coast
  • A unit placer in the debug window: pick an owner, filter the full type list, then place at the cursor or click repeatedly to lay units out — finished or as nanoframes — with a button to clear one owner's units
  • Frame stalls fixed: the ferry planner was re-scanning the whole map three times a second whenever it found nothing, dragging the sim from 30 to 7.8 ticks/s on a land map. Terrain reachability is now labelled once, metal searches walk an index of the patches, and RWE_AI_PROFILE=1 times every AI pass

Performance

  • Eight hundred units at 36 fps, up from eight. Both halves of the cost were found by measuring, and the guess would have been wrong — the renderer was never the problem. At small unit counts its apparent 92% of the frame was mostly vsync idle, which is exactly the sort of number that sends you optimising the wrong thing. Per-phase tick timing named two costs almost exactly equal: unit behaviour at 11.9 ms a tick and pathfinding at 11.4 ms, with visibility, COB, projectiles and the AI under 1.6 ms between them
  • Behaviour was 85% target acquisition — about 694 searches a tick, each walking all eight hundred units, growing 4.07× per doubling. It goes through a cell grid now, the same 128-world-unit shape the original's own radius visitor uses, rebuilt once a tick. The index can only ever return a superset: positions go stale as the pass moves units, so queries are widened by a margin and the real distance test still runs against the live unit, and candidates come back in unit id order so the random draws land on the same units in the same sequence. That it cannot change the outcome is the property the differential test pins — the same fight run twice for 150 ticks, once with the margin widened until it degenerates into the old whole-list walk, comparing every weapon's target and the whole simulation hash each tick
  • Pathfinding was not doing many searches: exactly four a tick, every one pinned at its thousand-expansion cap. The cost was per expansion, 2840 ns, some fifty times what a grid A* expansion should be — two hash maps where the open list wrote an entry per sift step, a heap key rebuilt from its parts on each of twenty comparisons, fifteen cell visits where eight would do, two allocations per expansion. Flat stamped arrays, a precomputed key and memoised neighbours: 477 ns. Routes are provably unchanged, because the old implementation was checked out, its exact vertex sequences captured, and the new one held to them
  • 400 units: 51 → 60 fps (vsync). 800 units: 8 → 36 fps. The phase timing stays, compiled out behind RWE_ENABLE_SIMPROF; the next round will want exactly the breakdown that found these two
  • Then the frame, and the moral above needed revising. “The renderer was never the problem” was true of the simulation pass and false a moment later: with the tick down to 4.2 ms it was holding nearly the whole frame. There was no render-side profiler to say so, so RWE_ENABLE_RENDERPROF was built to the same shape as the simulation one — whole frame, split by phase, with counts beside the milliseconds so a cost can be read against how much work produced it. The roadmap's standing claim of 23 ms did not survive it: the renderer measured 29.8 ms, and on a water map the largest cost in the frame was neither the sim nor the renderer but 127,000 wake particles, about half the frame split across both halves of it
  • Four things the breakdown named. Piece transforms were resolved by name every frame — a toUpper() allocation and a hash lookup per link of the parent chain, per piece, per unit, and again for the shadow pass — now resolved once (17.7 → 5.0 ms). Nothing was culled at all; the projection is orthographic, so the test is one transform and four comparisons, and shadows cull on where the shadow lands rather than where the unit is, so aircraft keep theirs (10,400 → 2,840 piece draws). Wake dots were all built and uploaded whether on screen or not, 758,000 vertices and 18 MB a frame (12.9 → 4.7 ms). And the particle update was ray-casting the terrain per dot per tick to find the shore, where the cells' own corner heights settle it over open water (10 → 3.6 ms a tick)
  • 800 ground units: 34.0 → 16.6 ms a frame, 29 → 60 fps, now on the vsync cap, with the world render itself 28.1 → 5.15 ms. 800 hover units: 84.9 → ~26 ms, 11 → ~40 fps. Nothing under sim/ was touched, because a desync is worse than a slow frame — MapTerrain::getHeightAt is the wrong algorithm for a height sample and was left alone for exactly that reason. Batching unit meshes by texture is still undone and the measurement does not justify it: at 2,840 piece draws the unit pass is 0.14 ms

Build and tooling

  • Windows checkouts without symlinks build (real asio include path); MinGW runtime DLLs copied next to the executables
  • Fixed the freeze under heavy fire: a deadlock between the render loop setting sound volumes and SDL_mixer's audio thread reporting finished tracks (track‑finished events are now queued for the main thread). RWE_DEBUG_SPAWN=ARMPW*12@0:8:1 reproduces it
  • A standing battle harness. battle_test spawns a fixed number of units for each of two players at opposite start positions, walks them at each other and replaces them as they die, with a live count slider in the debug panel so the fight can be pushed until something gives without restarting. It launches the real game — the same renderer, simulation and scene loop as rwe.exe, through the same run() — which is the only way its numbers mean anything, and which meant lifting the launch path out of main.cpp into a shared GameLaunch
  • Two headless probes. ui_probe builds the real UI panels from the real game data, dumps every gadget's hitbox and delivers clicks the way the scenes do, printing which gadget takes each event — it found three faults that play-testing could only gesture at. solar_probe reads a 3DO and replays the engine's shading pipeline over it, printing the row each polygon would get, which is how the vertex-normal convention was settled in one command rather than a play-test round
  • A black world with fog switched off turned out to be OpenGL refusing to draw: mapTerrain.frag has two samplers of different types, and the fog one was only assigned inside the if (fog) branch, so with no fog both sat on texture unit 0 — which fails draw-time validation, raises GL_INVALID_OPERATION and draws nothing. Nothing said a word, because the only error checks in the engine sit in the fog helpers that path skips
  • The D‑gun has an order behind it. The BLAST button rendered and gated itself correctly and nothing listened for the message it emitted, so a commandfire weapon — the one thing in the game that cannot be aimed by anything except an explicit order, which is the entire meaning of the bit — could not be fired at all. Read out of the exe first: the weapon is slot 2 by index and not by scanning for the flag, bare ground is a valid target, and one shot ends the order because any commandfire shot raises a different event bit that the attack handlers delete themselves on. The d quickkey came free with it; it had been bound to Guard, where the original has g
  • A patrol engages by ordering an attack. A patrolling aircraft was wiping its own attack run every tick — the order dispatcher drops the run for any order that is not an attack, so that a Move order can steer, and patrol engages by calling into the attack code itself. The bomber rebuilt its run from a standstill every tick and crawled forward at one tick of acceleration, never reaching a release point. It now does what the original does: a sighting becomes a real order in front of the patrol, with a move back to the spot where contact was made behind it, and a leash of maneuverleashlength from that spot — the first thing in RWE to read a field that had been parsed and ignored since April. Two rules came with it: the gate is Fire At Will exactly, not "anything but Hold Fire", and the break‑off radius is min(SightDistance, weapon‑0 range). Worth knowing and not a bug: a Thunder ships on Hold Fire and never attacks on patrol in the original either
  • A unit ordered to move sets off at once. This had been written down as a pathfinding budget problem — four searches a tick, all at their cap, a couple of hundred requests backlogged — and the budget was the wrong suspect. The original installs a two‑point path, the unit's own position and then the goal, and the unit is walking on the tick it was ordered; the real route overwrites the straight line when the scheduler reaches it. RWE installed nothing and stood still, which at four hundred a side is seconds of an army not moving. A bench (path_bench) was written to measure the budget properly first, and what it showed was that raising it buys a quarter off the queue for double the worst‑case tick
  • The goal is relaxed instead of the map exhausted. The original walks greedily at the target before it searches at all, following walls it meets, and then accepts any cell at least as close as that walk managed. A search for somewhere unreachable therefore costs one walk, where RWE walked its whole open list to prove the obvious — which with units a footprint apart in a crowd was two thirds of every search. Measured on one build with the pass on and off: exhausted searches 443 → 6, expansions over 600 ticks 149,199 → 32,292, and 116 units of 200 reaching their destination where 96 did
  • The Killed script is told how hard the blow was, instead of a constant 50 — so a unit finished off by a Peewee no longer comes apart exactly like one hit by a Big Bertha. The minimap's fog stopped asking the driver for a new texture on every visibility change. And a roadmap entry that claimed RWE spawns wrecks for shot‑down aircraft turned out to be wrong about RWE rather than about TA; there is a test pinning both halves now
  • A unit blown apart leaves rubble, or nothing. A shipped Killed script reads the severity as a three‑band ladder and writes a level back; the spawner walks the corpse feature's featuredead chain one step for each level above the first, and leaves nothing when the chain runs out. RWE ran the ladder and discarded the answer, so every wreck in the game was the level‑one wreck however the unit died. The part that looked hard — reading a local back out of a COB thread that may have stopped at a sleep — needed no mechanism at all: a finished thread is not reaped until the next pass over the scripts, so it is alive either way when the pass returns
  • No std::uniform_int_distribution is left in the simulation. Twelve draws remained, and its bias correction is implementation‑defined — two builds given the same seeded generator can draw different numbers, and because rejection consumes draws they stay out of step afterwards. One of them, the one a COB script's own rand goes through, had carried a FIXME: probably not consistent across platforms since before this branch. They are one helper now, and its tests pin both the value and the number of raw draws it costs against a copy of the same generator
  • A builder on patrol no longer goes looking for a fight. A unit that can repair is not given the Patrol mission in the original at all — it gets RepairPatrol, whose handlers never call the target search — so a construction unit walking a route clears wreckage and mends things and never breaks off to chase a tank
  • Two OTA keys turned out to be a phantom to‑do. ComputerMetal and ComputerEnergy are parsed by the original into its map record and never read back — those two stores are the only accesses to either field in the whole binary — and AIProfile is not even referenced. RWE parsing all three and using none is exactly what TA does
  • Headless‑safe logging for tests; the suite now stands at 398 cases and 58,596 assertions across orders, pathing, economy, visibility, alliances, skirmish options, burning, wreckage, transports, aircraft, bombing, save/load and AI
